What Is a Factor Rate? How MCA Pricing Works

Updated November 18, 2026 · 3 min read

If you have been offered a merchant cash advance (MCA), the price was probably quoted as a factor rate: a decimal such as 1.2, 1.35 or 1.45. It looks small and friendly. This guide explains what it means, how to turn it into dollars, and why you should never compare it directly with an interest rate.

A factor rate in one sentence

A factor rate is a multiplier. You multiply the amount you are advanced by the factor rate, and the result is the total you agree to repay.

Total payback = Advance amount × Factor rate

For example, $50,000 × 1.30 = $65,000. The $15,000 difference is the cost of the advance. Because it is a fixed dollar amount agreed up front, it does not shrink as you repay.

How an MCA is repaid

An MCA is usually structured as a purchase of part of your future receivables rather than a loan. Repayment typically happens in one of two ways:

  • Fixed debits: a set amount is withdrawn from your business bank account every business day or every week.
  • Percentage of sales (holdback): the provider takes an agreed share of your daily card sales until the total payback is reached.

Terms are usually short, commonly from a few months up to around a year and a half, depending on the provider and the deal.

What are typical factor rates?

Offers commonly fall somewhere between about 1.1 and 1.5, depending on the provider, your sales history, your industry and the risk the funder sees. A higher number is not automatically a bad deal and a lower one is not automatically a fair one, because the repayment term matters just as much as the factor.

Why a factor rate is not an interest rate

  • It has no time component. An interest rate is charged per year or per month. A factor rate is a one-time multiplier, so it says nothing about how long you have the money.
  • It is fixed at signing. The total payback usually does not change if you repay faster, unless your contract includes an early-payoff discount. Ask about this in writing.
  • You repay as you go. Because payments start right away, you never hold the full advance for the full term. The effective cost per dollar-year borrowed is higher than the headline number suggests.
Same factor, very different cost. A 1.30 factor rate repaid over 3 months works out to roughly 207% APR. Repaid over 12 months, it is roughly 52%. The factor rate did not change; the term did.

How to compare offers fairly

  1. Write down the total payback in dollars and the amount you will actually receive after fees.
  2. Note the payment amount and schedule and how large it is relative to your typical revenue.
  3. Convert each offer to an estimated APR so you can compare it with loans and lines of credit. Our APR calculation guide shows how, the factor rate to APR chart gives quick lookups, or you can use the calculator below.
