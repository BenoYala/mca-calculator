How to Calculate the APR of a Merchant Cash Advance

Updated November 18, 2026 · 3 min read

Merchant cash advance providers quote a factor rate, not an APR. To compare an MCA with a bank loan, a line of credit or another offer, you need to translate it. This guide walks through the math with a real example so you can check any offer yourself.

The example offer

  • Advance: $50,000
  • Factor rate: 1.30
  • Term: 6 months, repaid with daily debits on business days
  • Upfront fees: none (we add them later)

Step 1: Find the total payback and cost

Payback = 50,000 × 1.30 = $65,000
Cost    = 65,000 − 50,000 = $15,000

Step 2: Work out the payment

Assuming about 22 business days per month, six months is 132 payments.

Payment = 65,000 ÷ 132 = $492.42 per business day

Step 3: The shortcut that understates the cost

A common shortcut divides the cost by the advance and annualizes it: 15,000 ÷ 50,000 = 30% over half a year, or 60% a year. It is easy, but it assumes you keep the whole $50,000 for the whole six months. You do not. You hand money back every day, so the average amount you are using is much lower.

Step 4: Solve for the true periodic rate

The APR method used for loans asks: what interest rate per payment period makes the present value of all my payments equal to the money I received?

Received = Σ  Payment ÷ (1 + r)^k      for k = 1 … 132

There is no simple formula for r, so it is solved by iteration, which is what the calculator does. For this offer the daily rate is about 0.41%. Multiply by 252 payment days a year and you get an APR of roughly 104%. With daily compounding, the effective annual rate is about 183%.

Why the two numbers differ. The shortcut says 60%; the loan-style method says about 104%. The second is the better measure of what the money really costs because it accounts for when you pay it back.

Step 5: Add the fees

If the funder deducts a 5% fee from the advance, you receive $47,500 but still repay $65,000. The same payments now buy less money, so the estimated APR rises from about 104% to about 126%. Always compare offers on the money you actually receive.

Daily vs. weekly payments

Switching the same deal to weekly payments of $2,500 gives an APR of about 107%. It is similar because the total payback and term are the same; the schedule only shifts the timing slightly.

Limits of any estimate

  • If repayment is a percentage of sales, the real payment schedule will vary.
  • Extra fees, renewals or early-payoff terms change the math.
  • Providers and regulators may use specific methods for the APR they disclose, so numbers can differ slightly from ours.
