Why a low-looking factor rate can be so expensive

A merchant cash advance is not a loan. The provider buys a slice of your future sales at a discount and you repay it through fixed daily or weekly debits, or a share of your card sales. The price is set by the factor rate, a flat multiplier. Nothing in a factor rate says how long you have to repay, and that is the problem.

Take the default example above: a $65,000 payback on a $50,000 advance over six months. The cost is $15,000, which sounds like 30% of the advance. But you repay roughly every business day, so for most of the term you are using far less than $50,000. The estimated APR works out to about 104%. Stretch the same factor rate over twelve months and the APR falls to roughly half that, which is why term length matters as much as the factor rate itself.

What this estimate does not include

  • Percentage-of-sales repayment, where the daily amount rises and falls with your revenue.
  • Broker commissions, processing or ACH charges and any other fees not listed in the fee field.
  • Early payoff discounts or renewals that restructure the deal.

Use the result as a yardstick to compare offers, not as a substitute for the numbers in your contract. Our step-by-step APR guide shows the full math.

Frequently asked questions

Quick answers about factor rates, APR and how this calculator works.

What is a factor rate?

A factor rate is a multiplier, usually somewhere between about 1.1 and 1.5, that sets how much you repay on a merchant cash advance. Multiply the amount advanced by the factor rate to get the total payback. A $50,000 advance at a factor rate of 1.3 means you repay $65,000.

Is an MCA APR the same as a loan APR?

It follows the same idea: the annualized cost of the money you actually receive, taking into account when you repay it. But an MCA is legally structured as a purchase of future receivables rather than a loan, so providers are not always required to quote an APR. This calculator gives an estimate so you can compare an MCA with loans on a like-for-like basis.

Why is the APR so high when the factor rate looks small?

A factor rate has no time component. You pay the same fixed fee whether the term is three months or twelve, and you repay a little at a time, so you do not hold the full amount for the full term. Spreading a fixed cost over a short period and then annualizing it produces a large percentage.

How is the payment estimated?

We divide the total payback evenly across the number of payments: 22 business days per month for daily payments, or about 4.33 weeks per month for weekly payments. Some MCAs take a percentage of daily card sales instead, so your real payment can change from day to day.

How is the APR calculated here?

We solve for the periodic interest rate that makes the present value of all your payments equal to the money you actually receive after fees, then multiply by the number of payments per year. This is the same idea behind a loan APR. We also show the effective annual rate with compounding, and a simple flat annualized figure that some brokers quote.

Do MCA providers have to disclose an APR?

Not under federal consumer lending rules, which cover consumer credit. Some states, including California and New York, require certain commercial financing providers to disclose an estimated APR, and the rules vary by state, provider type and deal size. If an offer does not show one, you can ask for the total payback, the payment schedule and the net funds you will receive in writing.

Does this site store the numbers I enter?

No. The calculation runs in your browser, and the values you type are not sent to our servers.
